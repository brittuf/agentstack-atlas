Agents at Work #5: AI agents are getting job descriptions

Week of November 12–19, 2026 · about a 6-minute read

This week's theme: agents got passports in #1, budgets in #2, performance reviews in #3 and a front desk in #4. This week they got job descriptions. Wallarm, Pulumi and Cypris all launched agents that each own one defined piece of work, with their own permissions and a set amount of autonomy. Our deployment of the week shows why the approach pays off: split one big agent into a team of narrow ones.

Deployment of the week: PostNord's phone agents (built on ElevenAgents)

PostNord, the Nordic postal and logistics company, already answered every inbound customer call in Sweden with an AI phone system. That system hit a ceiling at about 45% of calls resolved without escalation. So PostNord replaced it with a multi-agent system on ElevenLabs' ElevenAgents platform. An orchestrator works out what each caller needs and routes the call to one of several specialized subagents. Per ElevenLabs' customer story (Nov 17):

  • Task completion rose from 45% to 60%. ElevenLabs calls that a 33% increase in AI resolution rate.
  • The old system was fully decommissioned in ~90 days.
  • The orchestrator routes each caller to one of four specialized subagents, including a dedicated tracking agent.
  • The rollout ran in four phases: an isolated weekend test, a hybrid phase with human handover, a weekday partial deployment, then full production.

Caveats: this is a vendor case study from ElevenLabs, which sells the platform. It doesn't define "task completion" or say how many calls were measured. ElevenLabs itself says "60% is a current reading, not a final number." The page also has publishing errors: its title and one quote refer to a different customer, so we used only the parts that are clearly about PostNord.

What makes it work: narrow jobs. Tracking questions, the most common ones, go to "a simpler, lower-cost setup" instead of one agent that does everything. Live calls are A/B tested between model configurations, and before each go/no-go decision both teams reviewed the calls from the phase before. ElevenLabs says Nordic expansion to Denmark, Norway and Finland is underway.

Source: ElevenLabs, Nov 17, 2026

This week in agentic AI

1. Wallarm launches seven "Agentic Operators" for application security (Nov 19). Each operator "owns a defined part of security operations." Radar investigates attack spikes, Sift separates real attacks from false alarms and fixes the rules behind them, Tune and Trim maintain policies and rulesets, Lens reviews API exposure, Bard writes the summaries, and Wally is the team captain you talk to. Teams set autonomy per application and per remediation action: approval for every action, only for sensitive changes, or not at all. Wallarm says red-teaming and virtual-patching operators are coming to early access in early 2027. Wallarm

2. Pulumi launches Pulumi Agents, "your agentic infrastructure team" (Nov 18). You define an agent's goal or tasks, the constraints it must respect and its permissions. Then it investigates incidents, plans changes or handles maintenance, and returns findings and pull requests for review. Each agent has its own identity and Pulumi Cloud role, separate from the person who created it. Agents can be defined in code, so changes to an agent's responsibilities and access get reviewed before they're applied. Tasks can start on request, from Datadog, Honeycomb or PagerDuty alerts, or on a schedule. Pulumi

3. Cypris launches Agent Fleet for R&D teams (Nov 17). Cypris lets R&D organizations deploy specialized agents for jobs such as regulatory intelligence, material scouting, formulation, competitive intelligence and technology commercialization. Each agent works under the organization's subject-matter experts, "with defined responsibilities, persistent memory, performance feedback." CEO Steve Hafif says the company is "building the infrastructure for R&D organizations to manage AI agents much like they manage teams today." Cypris via PR Newswire

4. Google Labs opens a waitlist for Stitch Loop (Nov 18). Google calls Stitch Loop "a long-running product agent" that connects to your codebase and live metrics. It suggests improvements, writes code in a sandbox and opens GitHub pull requests. It ships with three default loops (security, latency and accessibility), and you can add your own, such as checkout conversion. "Nothing merges until you review the PR." The waitlist is open globally, with priority for eligible Google AI Ultra users. Google

5. The flip side: Airlock Digital adds controls over what agents may execute (Nov 17). Airlock's Agentic Control checks agent actions on employee machines against company policy before they run, starting with Codex and Claude. When an action is denied, it tells the agent why and can suggest an alternative. Airlock's argument: agents that are simply blocked "evaluate alternatives and continue working toward their objective, no matter how risky that approach may be." The release cites an April 2026 Cloud Security Alliance report that 82% of organizations had unknown AI agents running in their environments. Airlock Digital via PR Newswire

The pattern (our take): the "one agent that does everything" pitch is fading. This week's launches look more like an org chart: named roles, their own access, a manager who approves the risky moves, and autonomy that grows as trust builds. That's good news for small teams, because a narrow job is easier to test, easier to price and easier to explain to a customer. PostNord's tracking agent doesn't have to be smart about everything. It has to be very good at one question. Airlock's release is the other half: an agent without a clear job will look for its own way to finish the task. Write the job down before the agent starts.

Tool spotlight: ElevenAgents

ElevenAgents is ElevenLabs' platform for voice and chat agents, and it's what PostNord built on. ElevenLabs says you can configure voice, language, knowledge base, system prompt and workflows, deploy across phone, web, WhatsApp, SMS and email, set guardrails, and run simulations and automated tests "before anything reaches a customer." It lists templates for triage, order support, front desk and FAQ agents. Per its pricing page, the Free plan includes 15 minutes of calls, paid plans start at $6 a month for 75 minutes, extra minutes cost $0.08, and LLM usage is billed on top. ElevenLabs says there's no limit on the number of agents on any plan.

A first build in the spirit of this week: a two-agent phone line for a local business. A triage agent answers and asks one question, then routes to an FAQ agent (hours, prices, service area, loaded from your website) or transfers the call to a human for anything else. How-to tip: write your agent a one-page job description

Before you build, fill in five lines. Each one comes from this week's launches:

  1. One job. Name the single task the agent owns ("answers parcel-tracking calls," not "handles support"). PostNord routes its most common question to its own agent.
  2. Inputs and tools. List exactly what it can read and which systems it can touch. Pulumi gives each agent its own role, separate from the person who built it, so it doesn't simply inherit your access.
  3. Approval rules. Decide which actions need a human. Wallarm's three settings work at any size: approve everything, approve only sensitive changes, or approve nothing. Start at "everything" and loosen the setting as trust builds.
  4. Handoff. Say who takes over when the agent is stuck, and how the customer gets to a person.
  5. How you'll judge it. Pick one number, such as completion rate, and roll out in phases. PostNord went from a weekend test to a hybrid phase with human handover, then partial weekday hours, then full production, and reviewed calls before each step.

Keep the page in version control or a shared doc, and change it on purpose, the way Pulumi has teams review edits to an agent's responsibilities before they apply.
